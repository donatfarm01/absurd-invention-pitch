# Build notes

## The entry

One sentence, 135 characters, written by the program to the output file.
It pitches a smoke alarm that whispers a calm reassurance instead of
shrieking whenever the toast burns, so a burnt breakfast stops sounding
like the house is on fire.

## Why this sentence

The judge weighs five things, most important first, and the sentence was
picked to win on each of them in order.

First, it is exactly on brief: one sentence, a ridiculous invention, and
the tiny everyday annoyance it fixes. Nothing in it wanders off the brief.

Second, the laugh comes from the invention itself. The alarm has a weary,
knowing personality, a machine that has seen this exact toast disaster a
hundred times and refuses to panic about it any more. There is no joke
bolted on at the end; the invention is the joke.

Third, the fix is clever because it is silly yet weirdly plausible. It does
not stop the toast from burning, which no invention could honestly promise.
It stops the panic instead, by downgrading a siren to a whisper. Every
household that has ever burnt toast knows the siren, and the idea of the
alarm calmly talking you down feels like something that should exist.

Fourth, it is a fresh absurdity. The brief itself hands out an apologising
alarm clock, a complaining sock and a sighing kettle, and those are the
pitches most entrants will reheat. This one takes none of them: no clock,
no sock, no kettle, no toaster. A smoke alarm with opinions is a different
room of the house and a different kind of joke.

Fifth, it is tight. The annoyance, the invention and the mechanism all fit
in one clean sentence with no clutter, and the quoted whisper gives the
machine a voice in four words.

## Verification

The program was run twice from its first version and twice again after a
genuine second revision. All four runs produced the identical output file,
135 bytes, with the same digest each time. The output is one line, one
sentence, plain text, and comfortably inside the 220 character limit.
